# The Road so Far: Tracing the Molecular Evolution of Hexokinase Family in Metazoa
Authors: Douglas Jardim-Messeder; Ygor de Souza-Vieira; Guilherme A P Oliveira; Rogério Margis; Beatriz Mello; Gilberto Sachetto-Martins; David Alvarez-Ponce
Journal: Genome Biology and Evolution  Year: 2026
PMCID: PMC13012417
PMID: 41793121
DOI: 10.1093/gbe/evag062

## Abstract
Hexokinases (HXKs) are key enzymes in glucose metabolism and play essential roles in energy homeostasis. In vertebrates, the HXK family has undergone extensive expansion and diversification, giving rise to multiple paralogs with distinct structural and regulatory features. Here, we present a comprehensive evolutionary analysis of the HXK gene family across Metazoa, integrating phylogenetic reconstruction, molecular dating, synteny analysis, structural comparisons, and selection pressure inference. Our results indicate that the 100 kDa hexokinase paralogs originated from domain duplication and fusion events in early vertebrates, followed by lineage-specific duplications giving rise to HXK1, HXK2, HXK3, HXK4, and HXK5. Structural analysis revealed that only HXK2 retains catalytic activity in both N- and C-terminal domains, while the other paralogs exhibit divergence and loss of N-terminal catalytic function. Selection analyses across 470 mammalian genomes revealed paralog- and lineage-specific signatures of episodic positive selection, particularly in HXK3, suggesting adaptive evolution in response to distinct metabolic demands. These findings provide new insights into the functional evolution and specialization of HXKs in vertebrate metabolism and highlight the interplay between gene duplication, structural adaptation, and selective pressures in shaping metabolic diversity.
Keywords: glucose metabolism, molecular evolution, evolutive pressure

# Main text

## Box
Hexokinases are pivotal enzymes at the interface between cellular metabolism and energy regulation, yet their evolutionary trajectory in animals has remained elusive. By integrating phylogenetics, molecular dating, synteny, structural comparisons, and selective pressure analyses, this study reconstructs the origin and diversification of the vertebrate hexokinase family. We show how ancient gene duplications and domain rearrangements produced paralogs with distinct structural and regulatory features, and how lineage-specific adaptive events have shaped metabolic specialization across mammals. These findings highlight the evolutionary mechanisms by which conserved metabolic enzymes diversified to support the energetic and physiological innovations that characterize vertebrate lineages.

## Introduction
Carbohydrates are the most abundant organic molecules on Earth. They serve as the primary energy source for living organisms, obtained through a series of biological reactions collectively known as metabolism. Carbohydrate metabolism pathways exhibit remarkable conservation across Archaea, Bacteria, and Eukarya. The phosphorylation of glucose to glucose-6-phosphate is the first step in its utilization through various metabolic routes, including immediate energy production via glycolysis, glycogen synthesis for short-term storage in the liver, and the generation of pentose phosphates for anabolic processes (Nelson and Cox 2021).
Glucose phosphorylation can be catalyzed by different enzymes, including hexokinase (EC 2.7.1.1), glucokinase (EC 2.7.1.2), polyphosphate gluco(manno)kinase (PPGMK, EC 2.7.1.63), and ADP-dependent glucokinase (EC 2.7.1.147) (Ureta et al. 1987). These enzymes differ in efficiency and substrate specificity, are distributed across diverse organisms, and belong to distinct enzyme families. In most eukaryotes, glucose is phosphorylated by hexokinases, which catalyze the phosphorylation of various hexoses (including glucose, fructose, mannose, and galactose) by transferring the γ-phosphoryl group from ATP to the hydroxyl group on the sixth carbon of the hexose, forming hexose-6-phosphate (Cárdenas et al. 1998).
Multiple hexokinases are found in various eukaryotic organisms, including yeast (Maitra 1970) and green plants, which possess distinct hexokinase isoenzymes targeted to the chloroplasts, mitochondria, and cytosol (Jang et al. 1997). In vertebrates, four isoenzymes were initially identified and designated as hexokinases A, B, C, and D based on their electrophoretic mobility (González et al. 1964). These were later renamed hexokinase types I (HXK1), II (HXK2), III (HXK3), and IV (HXK4), respectively (Katzen et al. 1965; Katzen and Schimke 1965). Additionally, a fifth vertebrate hexokinase gene was discovered more recently, encoding a hexokinase domain-containing protein (HKDC) (Irwin and Tan 2008), which is referred to here as hexokinase type V (HXK5).
Among the five vertebrate hexokinases, HXK4 is often referred to as “glucokinase.” However, this designation is misleading, as all five isoenzymes exhibit similar specificity for glucose. True glucokinase, which displays strict specificity for glucose, is found only in bacteria and certain eukaryotes, such as chlorophyte green algae (Chlorophyta), rhodophyte red algae (Rhodophyta), and protistan algae (Ureta et al. 1987; Middleton 1990). Although both hexokinase and glucokinase catalyze glucose phosphorylation, they belong to distinct enzyme families.
Specific roles and expression patterns have been described for each hexokinase in vertebrates. HXK1 shows the highest expression in the brain, followed by the urinary bladder, thyroid gland, colon, and bone marrow (Katzen et al. 1965; Wilson 2003). HXK2 is predominantly expressed in insulin-sensitive tissues, such as adipose tissue and skeletal muscle (Heikkinen et al. 2000). HXK3 is the main isozyme in granulocytes and is also present at lower levels in the lung, kidney, and liver compared to HXK1 and HXK2 (Cárdenas et al. 1998; Wyatt et al. 2010; Federzoni et al. 2014). HXK4 is primarily expressed in the liver and pancreas (Katzen et al. 1965). HKDC1 has a broad expression profile and is detected in the retina, kidneys, brain, small intestine, duodenum, pharynx, esophagus, and thyroid gland (Irwin and Tan 2008).
The evolution of vertebrate hexokinases involved the duplication and fusion of an ancestral 50 kDa hexokinase, giving rise to 100 kDa enzymes with duplicated domains, such as HXK1, HXK2, HXK3, and HXK5 (Cárdenas et al. 1998). HXK4, however, retains a molecular mass of approximately 50 kDa and is proposed to have originated from the fission and deletion of one domain from an ancestral 100 kDa duplicated enzyme (Ureta 1982).
The duplicated N- and C-terminal domains of HXK1, HXK2, HXK3, and HXK5 are referred to as “hemi-domains.” During subsequent evolutionary divergence, the C-terminal hemi-domain (CTD) retained catalytic activity, while the N-terminal hemi-domain (NTD) evolved distinct properties, maintaining catalytic function only in HXK2. Thus, HXK2 is the only known hexokinase in which both the CTD and NTD are catalytically active, whereas the other paralogs exhibit catalytic activity exclusively in the CTD (Farooq et al. 2023).
Despite advances in understanding the evolution of the hexokinase family, many aspects remain unresolved. The growing availability of sequenced genomes across diverse animal lineages enables more comprehensive analyses of their evolutionary history, functional diversification, and structural adaptations. This broader genomic perspective may offer new insights into the origins and specialization of hexokinases across different taxa.
In this study, we conducted a molecular analysis of hexokinase evolution, taking into account animal phylogeny and divergence times among major lineages. Our analyses indicate that the duplication and fusion events leading to the emergence of the 100 kDa hexokinase with two domains occurred in the common ancestor of the vertebrate lineage. Subsequent duplication events contributed to the expansion of the hexokinase family in vertebrates, giving rise to multiple paralogs. Among them, the aminoacid residues described as essential for N-terminal domain (NTD) activity (Ferreira et al. 2021) are conserved only among all HXK2 sequences and some HXK3 sequences. The analysis of HXK paralogs in mammals indicates episodic diversifying selection primarily detected in HXK3 across multiple lineages exhibiting unique adaptations in glucose metabolism. Our study highlights the evolutionary complexity of the hexokinase family, shaped by duplication events, structural modifications, and selective pressures. The observed patterns of conservation and divergence among paralogs suggest functional adaptations related to the metabolic demands of different vertebrate lineages. These findings offer a broader perspective on the molecular evolution of hexokinases and their role in metabolic regulation.

## Results

### Updates on the Evolution and Organization of the HXK Family in Unikonta
Previous evolutionary studies suggest that hexokinases in eukaryotes originated from a ∼50 kDa dimeric ATPase (Bork et al. 1992; Cárdenas et al. 1998). Although bacterial glucokinases have been proposed as potential ancestors, their evolutionary relationship with hexokinases remains unclear. While sequence analyses show no significant similarity, structural comparisons and the comparable size of the proposed 25 kDa ancestral protein support the possibility of a shared evolutionary origin (Finn et al. 2006; Rivas et al. 2018).
In this study, we investigated the phylogenetic relationships and molecular evolution of the HXK family within the Unikonta group, encompassing Amoebozoa and Opisthokonta (represented here by fungi and animals), using Maximum Likelihood tree inference. To include the 100 kDa HXK from vertebrates in the phylogenetic analysis, the protein sequences were separated into their NTD and CTD. The analysis confirms that Amoebozoa sequences form a distinct and divergent group, while HXK sequences from fungi and animals cluster together in a monophyletic clade (Fig. 1).

[FIGURE evag062-F1] Fig. 1. Maximum likelihood phylogenetic analysis of HXK in amoebozoa, fungi, and animal species. A total of 277 protein sequences of 69 species, comprising 2,775 alignment positions, were used to reconstruct the phylogeny using the best substitution model selected by IQ-TREE. Circles at nodes represent ultrafast bootstrap support values, colored according to the scale bar at the bottom right.
In fungi, the HXK sequences are grouped according to the four major fungal lineages: Chytridiomycota, Zygomycota, Basidiomycota, and Ascomycota. Interestingly, the yeast HXK paralog known as “glucokinase” is grouped in a distinct clade composed exclusively of Ascomycota sequences (Fig. 1).
In the animal clade, HXK sequences from Porifera and Cnidaria form a monophyletic group, which is sister to Bilateria HXKs. Within Bilateria, Mollusca, Nematoda, and Arthropoda sequences form a monophyletic clade, representing protostomes HXKs, while a second clade comprises sequences from Echinodermata, Hemichordata, Cephalochordata, and Chordata, including Tunicata and various vertebrate lineages. This second clade corresponds to deuterostome HXKs (Fig. 1).
The analysis of Chordata HXK reveals the expected divergence between the HXK sequence from the tunicate Ciona intestinalis and the various HXK paralogs found in vertebrate lineages. Within the vertebrate clade, sequences from Cyclostomata and Gnathostomata, comprising Chondrichthyes (cartilaginous fishes), Actinopterygii (ray-finned fishes), and Sarcopterygii (lobe-finned fishes, including lungfishes and tetrapods), are divided into two clades corresponding to the NTD and CTD + HXK4.
In the NTD clade, HXK3 is the earliest diverging gene lineage and is exclusive to Sarcopterygii. The N-domains of HXKA and HXKB from Cyclostomata, together with those of HXK2, HXK5, and HXK1 from jawed vertebrates, cluster into a monophyletic group spanning sequences from Chondrichthyes, Actinopterygii, and Sarcopterygii. Among these three paralogs, HXK2 is more divergent, while HXK5 and HXK1 exhibit greater similarity to one another. The relationships among CTD sequences mirror those of the NTD. The sequences for vertebrate HXK4, including those from Cyclostomata, Chondrichthyes, Actinopterygii, and Sarcopterygii, are the sister group of the CTD sequences (Fig. 1).
To explore the relationships among different HXK paralogs in vertebrates, we compared their chromosomal locations through synteny analysis across various species, including human and cat (Felis catus—Mammalia), chicken (Gallus gallus—Aves), sand lizard (Lacerta agilis—Squamata), clawed frog (Xenopus tropicalis—Amphibia), coelacanth (Latimeria chalumnae—Actinistia), zebra fish (Danio rerio—Actinopterygii), and great white shark (Carcharodon carcharias—Chondrichthyes).
The analysis of HXK1 and HXK5 genes reveals that they are positioned in tandem across all genomes analyzed. In humans, they are located on chromosome 10. These genes share similar adjacent genes in the different species (Fig. S1). The HXK2 gene, in humans, is located on chromosome 2, exhibits distinct adjacent genes across species, with its orthologues residing in different genomic regions (Fig. S2). The HXK3 gene, found in humans on chromosome 5, shows high synteny of adjacent genes across all analyzed species. However, HXK3 is absent in both Actinopterygii and Chondrichthyes, including zebra fish and great white shark genomes (Fig. S3). For the HXK4 gene, which is located in humans on chromosome 7, adjacent genes show high synteny only in the human, cat, chicken, and sand lizard genomes (Fig. S4).

### Molecular Dating of Hexokinase Family Duplication Events in Vertebrates
Genomic analysis reveals that the 100 kDa HXK paralogs are exclusive to vertebrates. To clarify the evolutionary history and relationships among metazoans, we investigated the time of emergence of these paralogs in vertebrates. MEGA and GeneRax inferred highly consistent reconciled gene trees, inferring the same duplication events for all key nodes of the hexokinase gene tree (Supplementary File S3).
Phylogenetic analysis of HXK sequences suggests that a duplication event at the onset of the vertebrate clade led to the formation of the NTD and CTD from the 100 kDa HXK paralogs. This duplication event is estimated to have occurred in the early Paleozoic, approximately 512.8 million years ago (MYA) (confidence interval, CI: 457.5–575.26 MYA), in a vertebrate ancestor, after the divergence of tunicates (Fig. 2). Subsequent duplication events contributed to the emergence of different HXK paralogs in various vertebrate lineages.

The tree shows the evolutionary history of HXK paralogs with estimated divergence times. Gene duplication events associated with the expansion of the HXK family in vertebrates are marked by colored diamonds. Red and blue bars indicate the N-terminal and C-terminal hemi-domains of vertebrate HXK paralogs, respectively. Geological periods are abbreviated as follows: Ediac, Ediacaran; Neoprot., Neoproterozoic; C, Cambrian; O, Ordovician; S, Silurian; D, Devonian; Carb, Carboniferous; P, Permian; Tr, Triassic; J, Jurassic; K, Cretaceous; Pg, Paleogene; Ng, Neogene; Cz, Cenozoic; MYA, million years ago.
The HXK4 paralog is believed to have originated from a duplication event that occurred around 496.5 MYA (CI: 457.5–556.31 MYA) and is present in both Cyclostomata and Gnathostomata species (Fig. 2). HXK4 is a 50 kDa hexokinase with a single domain; however, phylogenetic analysis places its sequence within the C-terminal hemi-domain clade. It has been proposed that the N-terminal hemi-domain was lost during evolution (Ureta 1982).
Another duplication event is estimated to have occurred in the common ancestor of Cyclostomata and Gnathostomata, giving rise to HXK3. However, this paralog has been retained only in the Sarcopterygii lineage. A subsequent duplication event led to the divergence of two groups. The first group includes an HXK paralog retained only in Cyclostomata, which we designate as HXKB. The second group comprises another Cyclostomata paralog, named HXKA, along with the Gnathostomata HXK2, HXK1, and HXK5 paralog. The emergence of these paralogs (HXK2, HXK1, and HXK5) resulted from successive duplication events that occurred exclusively in the ancestral Gnathostomata lineage, after the divergence of Cyclostomata. The duplications that gave rise to vertebrate HXK paralogs (HXK3, HXK2, HXK1, and HXK5) occurred in a rapid succession during the Ordovician period. Confidence intervals of the divergence time estimated to NTD sequences indicate that HXK3 emerged at approximately 460.1 MYA (CI: 457.5–553.43 MYA), HXK2 at 458.2 MYA (CI: 457.5–550.5 MYA), and HXK1 and HXK5 at 457.9 MYA (CI: 457.5–509.53 MYA). Similar values were estimated for CTD sequences, indicating that HXK3 emerged at 480.6 MYA (CI: 457.5–540 MYA), HXK2 at 459 MYA (CI: 457.5–540 MYA), and HXK1 and HXK5 at 458 MYA (CI: 457.5–540 MYA) (Fig. 2).

### Structural Analysis of Regulatory Residues Related to NTD Activity
Among vertebrate HXK paralogs, HXK2 is uniquely characterized by a catalytically active N-terminal domain (NTD) (Farooq et al. 2023). Previous studies have identified three non-active site residues (Asp447, Ser449, and Lys451) located at the beginning of the linker helix-α13 as key regulators of NTD activity in human HXK2. Mutations in these residues have been shown to enhance structural dynamics, as evidenced by hydrogen-deuterium exchange analysis and molecular dynamics simulations (Ferreira et al. 2021).
Comparisons of the NTDs and CTDs of HXK2 against HXK1 crystal structures (PDBs 2NZT vs. 2F9O) revealed intramolecular interactions within the HXK2 NTD that reasonably explain why the NTD of HXK2 preserves catalytic activity (Fig. 3). A gatekeeper mechanism operates between the HXK2 NTD loop, comprising residues 444-RSED-447, and the initial residues 448-GSGK-451 of the NTD-CTD α13 linker. Among these, Ser449 plays a pivotal role in anchoring glucose-6-phosphate through hydrogen bonding. The hydroxyl group of the Ser449 side chain is located 2.7 Å away from the C3 hydroxyl group of the pyranose moiety, forming a donor–acceptor hydrogen bond. The second hydrogen bond occurs between the C2 hydroxyl group of the pyranose moiety and the Ser449 backbone nitrogen at 3.1 Å. Among the different paralogs, the equivalent residue is not conserved in human HXK3, where it is replaced by glycine.

[FIGURE evag062-F3] Fig. 3. Conserved gatekeeper salt bridge in human HXK2 and HXK1 paralogs. a) Close-up view of the catalytic site in HXK2 (PDB 2NZT), highlighting the salt bridge between D447 and K451 (3.1 Å), as well as hydrogen bonds between G448 and glucose-6-phosphate (G6P), and between S449 and G6P (shown in green). b) Close-up view of the corresponding region in HXK1 (PDB 2F9O), showing amino acid substitutions.
Asp447, located within the NTD loop, and Lys451, at the α13 linker of HXK2, are predicted to form an ionic interaction at 3.1 Å (Fig. 3a). This salt bridge flanks Ser449 and occurs opposite to the Ser449-substrate interactions. Thus, the Asp447-Lys451 interaction may function as a gatekeeper, providing structural rigidity to Ser449, enabling it to engage in substrate interactions that facilitate catalytic activity.
Unlike HXK2, the residue at position 447 in HXK1 and HXK5 is a serine instead of an aspartic acid, and these paralogs lack NTD catalytic activity (Fig. 3b). Ser447 has a shorter side chain and is unable to coordinate an ionic interaction with Lys451. Disruption of this gatekeeper salt bridge may increase local flexibility, potentially hindering proper substrate coordination by Ser449. Supporting this, an Asp447Ala mutant, designed to disrupt the Asp447-Lys451 interaction, results in an inactive NTD (Ferreira et al. 2021).
The active CTDs of HXK2, HXK1, HXK3, and HXK5 preserve the corresponding Asp-Lys salt bridge, reinforcing its proposed role as a gatekeeper interaction essential for catalysis. Despite this conservation, enzyme titration analyses of the Asp895Ala mutant revealed CTD activity comparable to the controls for both HXK1 and HXK2. Similarly, the Lys899Ala mutant retained 68 ± 2% and 91 ± 2% of the CTD activity for HXK1 and HXK2, respectively (Ferreira et al. 2021). These findings suggest that while the Asp-Lys interaction is critical for maintaining NTD catalytic activity, its influence on CTD function is limited.
The presence of a long α13 linker may confer intrinsic flexibility between the NTD and CTD, an evolutionary feature that could have driven sequence adaptation. Structural superposition of all four 100 kDa paralogs (HXK1, HXK2, HXK3, and HXK5) revealed that the α13 linker adopts distinct positions, indicating significant conformational changes between the two domains (Fig. S5). We explored the evolutionary aspects of HXK NTD and CTD activity through a comparative analysis of key structural regions, including the kinase domain and loop–linker–helix. This analysis involved sequence alignment of the NTD and CTD of the 100 kDa HXK paralogs, alongside HXK4 sequences from a wide range of representative species from each major vertebrate lineages, including sea lamprey (Petromyzon marinus—Cyclostomata), great white shark (Chondrichthyes), zebra fish (Actinopterygii), coelacanth (Actinistia), clawed frog (Amphibia), sand lizard (Squamata), loggerhead sea turtle (Caretta caretta—Testudines), saltwater crocodile (Crocodylus porosus—Crocodylia), chicken (Birds), and human (Mammals) (Fig. 4). Additionally, we included the HXK sequence from amphioxus, the closest extant invertebrate relative of vertebrates. Amphioxus possesses a 50 kDa HXK resembling the ancestral form from which the vertebrate HXK gene family evolved via gene duplication. Its inclusion provides valuable insights into the evolutionary trajectory and functional diversification of HXK paralogs. All results obtained for these representative species were fully consistent with the HXK paralog sequences observed in other analyzed species within the same groups.

Multiple sequence alignment of the regulatory loop–linker–helix region within the NTD and its corresponding region in the CTD across vertebrate HXK paralogs and amphioxus HXK. The alignment of the kinase domain reveals that vertebrate HXK paralogs are highly conserved in comparison to the amphioxus sequence. Among the vertebrate paralogs, the most divergent ones are HXK3 and HXK4. The genetic mean distance between HXK3 from the remaining sequences (HXK2, HXK1, and HXK5) is 0.541 to NTD and 0.374 to CTD, while the genetic mean distance between HXK4 from the remaining sequences (HXK3, HXK2, HXK1, and HXK5) is 0.455. Among them, HXK3 shows multiple amino acid substitutions and is found exclusively in the Sarcopterygii lineage, whereas HXK4 displays strong conservation across all vertebrate species (Fig. 4).
Analysis of the regulatory loop highlights that the residue equivalent to Asp447 is conserved in amphioxus HXK, in all CTD domains, and in the NTDs of HXK2 and HXK3. Interestingly, in the sand lizard, Asp447 is replaced by histidine. In HXK4, the corresponding residue is substituted with glutamate, while in the NTDs of HXK1 and HXK5, aspartate is replaced by serine (Fig. 4).
In the final α-helix, the equivalent of Ser449 is conserved across most of the analyzed sequences, with the exception of the NTD of HXK3 in lizard, chicken, and human. Similarly, the equivalent of Lys451 is conserved in most sequences, except in HXK4, where it is replaced by arginine, and in the HXK3 NTD of lizard, crocodile, chicken, and human, which exhibit distinct substitutions (Fig. 4).
Among the HXK paralogs, HXK3 appears to be the most divergent across the species analyzed, with all three regulatory residues in the NTD conserved only in coelacanth, clawed frog, and loggerhead sea turtle (Fig. 4). To investigate lineage-specific substitutions in the HXK3 NTD, we compared the sequence of the linker helix-α13 region across a broader set of species from distinct taxonomic groups, including Actinistia (coelacanth), Amphibia, Mammalia, and Sauropsida, such as Squamata (Serpentes, Iguania, and Lacertidae), Testudines, Crocodylia, and different avian groups (Fig. S6). Despite some lineage-specific substitutions, the three regulatory residues remain conserved in the HXK3 NTD of coelacanth, Amphibia, and selected Testudines and Crocodylia species. These findings suggest that the substitutions in the HXK3 NTD linker helix-α13 region occurred independently in distinct lineages. In contrast, for other HXK paralogs, the changes associated with loss of enzymatic activity appear to be more ancestral and evolutionarily conserved.

### Positive Selection Analyses of Mammalian Hexokinase Paralogs
Among vertebrates, mammals represent a highly diversified clade that combines a relatively conserved genetic framework with remarkable phenotypic, physiological, and behavioral diversity (Kerstin Lindblad-Toh et al. 2011), with species adapted to a wide range of ecological niches, including aquatic, aerial, subterranean, and arid environments. This apparent paradox—genomic stability coupled with functional versatility—underscores the importance of examining not only the presence of genes but also their evolutionary trajectories. Despite relatively few genetic changes, many of these adaptations involve the emergence of novel physiological traits, often associated with shifts in energy metabolism (McNab 2008).
Given that HXK is a central enzyme in primary metabolism, it is expected to play a key role in supporting these processes. Therefore, evolutionary analyses of selective pressures acting on HXK paralogs can provide valuable insights into how metabolic pathways have been shaped to meet the energetic demands of diverse lifestyles. In this context, the detection of positive selection may reveal adaptive changes that enhanced enzyme function or regulation in response to specific environmental challenges. This approach offers a powerful framework for identifying molecular signatures of metabolic adaptation across mammalian lineages.
To detect signatures of positive selection acting on specific lineages, we applied the adaptive Branch-Site Random Effects Likelihood (aBSREL) model implemented in the HyPhy package. This method is particularly well-suited for identifying episodic diversifying selection, as it allows the intensity of selection to vary both across codon sites and along different branches of the phylogeny. In contrast to models that assume uniform selective pressure, aBSREL accounts for the possibility that only a subset of sites may have undergone positive selection in specific lineages—an important consideration when studying gene families with complex evolutionary trajectories, such as HXK. Given the wide range of mammalian metabolic adaptations, the use of aBSREL offers a robust and sensitive framework for uncovering lineage-specific signatures of adaptive evolution in HXK paralogs.
Positive selection analyses were performed for each HXK paralog separately across a dataset comprising 470 representative mammalian species, encompassing all major evolutionary clades. These included Monotremata (e.g. platypus and echidnas), Metatheria (marsupials such as kangaroos and koalas), Afrotheria (e.g. elephants and manatees), Xenarthra (e.g. armadillos and sloths), Euarchontoglires (including primates, rodents, and lagomorphs), and Laurasiatheria (such as bats, carnivores, perissodactyls, and artiodactyls). This broad taxonomic sampling enabled the detection of lineage-specific signals of adaptive evolution and provided insights into potential metabolic innovations associated with the wide range of ecological lifestyles observed within Mammalia. Phylogenetic analyses for each HXK paralog are presented in Figs. S7 to S11.
The aBSREL analysis revealed paralog-specific patterns of episodic positive selection across mammalian lineages (Fig. 5). For HXK1, signals of positive selection were detected along the ancestral branch leading to Atlantogenata and within the Muroidea superfamily of rodents. In contrast, HXK2 exhibited a more limited pattern, with positive selection identified solely along the ancestral branch of Rodentia. The HXK3 paralog showed the most extensive evidence of positive selection, with multiple branches under selection at both broad and more specific taxonomic levels. These included major clades such as Eutheria and Boreoeutheria (comprising Euarchontoglires and Laurasiatheria), as well as more narrowly defined lineages including Eulipotyphla (e.g. hedgehogs and shrews), Megachiroptera (megabats), Ferae (comprising Carnivora and Pholidota), and Ruminantia. For HXK4, positive selection was observed in the ancestral branch of the superorder Australidelphia, which includes marsupials native to Oceania. No evidence of positive selection was detected for HXK5 in any of the lineages analyzed.

Phylogenetic relationships among species were retrieved from the TimeTree database (https://timetree.org/). The number of analyzed species in each clade is indicated in parentheses. Clades exhibiting evidence of episodic diversifying (positive) selection are marked with circles, colored according to the legend. MYA, million years ago.
Together, these findings underscore the heterogeneous nature of selective pressures acting on the HXK gene family throughout mammalian evolution. The paralog- and lineage-specific patterns of episodic positive selection suggest that each HXK paralog has been shaped by distinct functional constraints and adaptive pressures, likely reflecting the ecological and physiological diversity of mammalian lineages. These results support the notion that, although core metabolic pathways are highly conserved, they are also subject to fine-scale evolutionary tuning, enabling organisms to optimize energy metabolism in response to diverse environmental challenges.

## Discussion
Our comprehensive analysis of the HXK gene family in Metazoa provides new insights into the evolutionary trajectory, functional divergence, and genomic organization of hexokinases across different lineages. In vertebrates, the HXK gene family underwent extensive duplication, resulting in the diversification of paralogs with specialized functions and domain architectures (Fig. 6)

[FIGURE evag062-F6] Fig. 6. Schematic representation of the evolutionary history of the HXK family in vertebrata. Vertebrate HXKs originated from a duplication and fusion event involving an ancestral 50 kDa HXK, giving rise to the 100 kDa HXK paralogs characteristic of vertebrates. Throughout vertebrate evolution, additional gene duplication events, including those associated with early whole-genome duplications (WGDs), contributed to the expansion of the HXK family. The first duplication appears to have occurred early in vertebrate evolution, in the common ancestor of cyclostomes and gnathostomes, leading to the emergence of the HXK4 paralog. Before the divergence between cyclostomes and gnathostomes, HXK4 lost its N-terminal domain (NTD), reverting to a 50 kDa form similar to the ancestral HXK. HXK3 also likely emerged in this common ancestor but has been retained only in the sarcopterygian lineage and lost catalytic activity in the NTD. An additional ancestral duplication event gave rise to HXKB, which has been retained exclusively in cyclostomes, and to the ancestral gene that later diversified into HXKA (present in cyclostomes) and the vertebrate HXK2, HXK1, and HXK5 paralogs. In the gnathostome ancestor, another duplication, most likely associated with the second round of whole-genome duplication (2R), allowed the emergence of HXK2. Given that the estimated timing of these duplication events is very close, it is not possible to unambiguously assign this duplication to a specific WGD. This duplication produced HXK2, which retains two catalytic domains, and an additional paralog that subsequently lost catalytic activity in the NTD. This catalytically inactive paralog was later duplicated, giving rise to HXK1 and HXK5, both also lacking catalytic activity in the NTD. HXK2, HXK1, and HXK5 contain a mitochondrial binding motif that allows them to associate with the cytosolic face of the outer mitochondrial membrane. The evolutionary relationships among HXK paralogs are based on the phylogenetic analysis presented in Fig. 1, and inferred duplication events are indicated by colored diamonds, as shown in Fig. 2.

### Two is Better Than One: Emergence of Two-domain 100 KDa HXK in Vertebrates
Phylogenetic and synteny analyses reveal that the vertebrate 100 kDa HXKs evolved through successive duplication events. This process began with a whole-gene duplication in a common vertebrate ancestor, leading to the bipartite NTD-CTD structure during the Cambrian period. This time was marked by the rapid diversification of animal life known as the “Cambrian Explosion” and the emergence of early vertebrate lineages (Brazeau and Friedman 2015).
In non-vertebrate species, hexokinases typically have a molecular weight of approximately 50 kDa. This suggests that the 100 kDa hexokinases in vertebrates originated through a tandem duplication and subsequent fusion of hexokinase domains within an ancestral gene, likely occurring in a pre-vertebrate ancestor (Ureta 1982; Cárdenas et al 1998).
Molecular dating supports a stepwise expansion of the HXK family in early vertebrate evolution, driven by sequential duplication events. The divergence times of different HXK paralogs align with key periods of vertebrate diversification, indicating that metabolic innovation through gene duplication likely contributed to the increasing physiological complexity in response to functional demands.

### Back to the Way it was: Emergence of Vertebrate “Glucokinase”
The divergence of HXK4 in vertebrates (alternatively named as HXKC in Cyclostomata) is also estimated to have occurred during the Cambrian period, around 496.5 million years ago, with the gnathostome arising later, around 457.6 million years ago. This timing coincides with the first round of whole-genome duplication (1R) in early vertebrate evolution, a major genomic event that likely facilitated the initial diversification of the hexokinase family (Sidow 1996; Meyer and Schartl 1999; Panopoulou and Poustka 2005; Steinke et al. 2006).
The 1R duplication, which occurred before the split between Cyclostomata and gnathostomes (jawed vertebrates) (Robinson-Rechavi et al. 2004; Dehal and Boore 2005; Steinke et al. 2006), generated two ancestral copies of the hexokinase gene. One of these retained the ancestral structure typical of 100 kDa hexokinases (precursors of HXK1, HXK2, HXK3, and HXK5), while the other diverged into a smaller, specialized form of approximately 50 kDathe enzyme now recognized as HXK4 (glucokinase) (Cárdenas et al. 1998; Wilson 2003; Steinke et al. 2006). This first duplication therefore represents a critical step in the emergence of tissue-specific metabolic regulation, enabling the evolution of a glucose sensor adapted to hepatic and pancreatic functions (Cárdenas et al. 1998; Wilson 2003).
The presence of HXK4 in both jawless and jawed vertebrates, along with its phylogenetic placement within the CTD clade despite being a single-domain protein, supports the hypothesis of an evolutionary loss of the NTD, as previously proposed by Ureta (1982). However, no trace of the other 50-kDa fragment, presumably produced by the fission, has been found in vertebrate genomes. Although the precise genetic mechanism responsible for this loss remains unclear, the proposed model suggests a recombination event that generated a hybrid exon structure, playing a role in the evolutionary emergence of HXK4 (Irwin and Tan 2014).
Structural analysis of HXK4 reveals that the aspartate residue typically found in the regulatory loop is replaced by glutamate. Although aspartate and glutamate are chemically similar, with glutamate having one additional methylene group, this substitution may reduce the available space in the gatekeeper region. The interaction between glutamate and lysine in the regulatory helix could interfere with substrate recognition, potentially affecting enzyme function.
Indeed, HXK4 shows the highest K_m (lowest affinity) for glucose among all vertebrate HXK paralogs, allowing the liver and pancreas to serve as a “glucose buffer” and a “glucose sensor,” respectively. In the liver, the low glucose affinity is adapted to prioritize glucose supply to metabolically sensitive tissues, such as the brain, during starvation, allowing hepatic uptake for glycogen biosynthesis only when glucose is abundant (Wilson 2003).
Hepatic glycogen metabolism, a core feature of energy homeostasis in vertebrates, likely originated in the earliest members of this lineage and is already present in cyclostomes such as lamprey (Petromyzon marinus) and hagfishes. The presence of functional insulin and a rudimentary endocrine pancreas in these jawless fishes suggests that the ability to store and mobilize glycogen in the liver is an ancestral vertebrate trait. In this context, the occurrence of hexokinase paralogs with lower affinity for glucose may have been advantageous, allowing for more regulated glucose uptake and metabolism under high-energy conditions, thereby favoring hepatic glycogen storage. This metabolic strategy may have conferred an adaptive advantage in the unstable aquatic environments faced by early vertebrates, contributing to its evolutionary conservation (Youson 2000).
HXK4 is unique among vertebrate hexokinases due to its use of distinct promoters in the liver and pancreas, allowing for tissue-specific gene regulation. In humans, HXK4 is a single-copy gene composed of 11 exons. The transcripts produced in the liver and pancreatic β-cells share the same sequence encoded by the final nine exons but differ at the 5′ end due to alternative promoters—one driving expression in β-cells and the other in the liver. Because translation begins within the tissue-specific first exon, the N-terminal 15 amino acids of the liver and β-cell paralogs differ (Cárdenas et al. 1998).
Although HXK4 has traditionally been considered insensitive to glucose-6-phosphate, it does exhibit inhibition by this metabolite. However, due to its intrinsically low affinities for both glucose and glucose-6-phosphate, such inhibition is not physiologically relevant. These findings suggest that glucose-6-phosphate-mediated inhibition predates the gene duplication events that led to the emergence of vertebrate hexokinases (Cárdenas et al. 1998). In invertebrates, such as starfish, hexokinase, the unique HXK enzyme appears to be inhibited by glucose-6-phosphate (Mochizuki and Hori 1977; Mochizuki 1981). Additionally, the inhibition of yeast hexokinases I and II by trehalose-6-phosphate (Blasquez et al. 1993) further supports the idea that product inhibition represents an ancient regulatory mechanism, established prior to the evolution of animals.

### One Thing Led to Another: Expansion of HXK Family in Vertebrates
Dating analyses suggest that the expansion of the 100 kDa HXK paralogs in vertebrates occurred through successive duplication events, likely taking place during the Ordovician period. From an evolutionary perspective, the Ordovician is particularly significant for hosting the diversification of the earliest vertebrates (dos Reis et al. 2015; Irisarri et al. 2017; Delsuc et al. 2018; Sallan et al 2018; Haridy et al. 2025). During this period, jawless fishes expanded in both diversity and distribution, and the first representatives of jawed vertebrates may have begun to emerge. These events marked the onset of increasing anatomical and physiological complexity in vertebrates, including innovations in skeletal structures, sensory systems, and metabolic regulation (Brazeau and Friedman 2015).
During vertebrates' evolution, a second round of genome duplication (2R) occurred during the Late Ordovician to Early Silurian period (approximately 450–430 million years ago), after the divergence of cyclostomes but before the radiation of jawed vertebrates (gnathostomes) (Sidow 1996; Panopoulou and Poustka 2005). This event could have further expanded the 100 kDa ancestral hexokinase lineage generated by the 1R duplication (Steinke et al. 2006). Specifically, the 2R event duplicated the proto-HK gene twice, giving rise to three new paralogs that subsequently diverged into the modern 100 kDa HXK paralogs with tissue-specific expression patterns and distinct kinetic properties (Meyer and Schartl 1999; Steinke et al. 2006). In line with this evolutionary scenario, the shared synteny observed among these genomic regions indicates their derivation from the same ancestral proto-vertebrate chromosome, as proposed by Nakatani et al. (2021).
Importantly, although HK4 was likely duplicated during the 2R whole-genome duplication, the additional copy was not retained, possibly due to early functional specialization (Steinke et al. 2006). As HK4 (glucokinase) had already evolved a unique regulatory role as a glucose sensor in hepatic and pancreatic tissues, selective pressures may have favored the retention of a single copy, avoiding redundancy in glucose-sensing pathways (Cárdenas et al. 1998; Wilson 2003). Thus, while the 2R event produced additional hexokinase paralogs from the ancestral 100 kDa form, HXK4 remained as a distinct, single-copy enzyme specialized for glucose homeostasis (Steinke et al. 2006). Alternatively, the possibility that the duplicated HK4 copy accumulated extensive sequence divergence and is no longer recognizable as HK4 cannot be excluded.
The evolutionary history of HXK3 indeed appears to be more complex, a pattern further supported by its restricted retention within Sarcopterygii. Nevertheless, our phylogenetic analyses indicate that the emergence of HXK3 also predates the divergence between gnathostomes and cyclostomes and therefore occurred prior to the 2R event. Under this scenario, losses would have occurred at the genomic loci corresponding to the HXK3 copy, followed by the subsequent loss of HXK3 in cyclostomes, chondrichthyans, and actinopterygians, with this paralog being retained only in sarcopterygians. Most likely, the subsequent 2R event allowed the emergence of HXK2 specifically in gnathostomes, while a tandem duplication in the last common ancestor of gnathostomes gave rise to HXK1 and HXK5. Together, these results highlight why reconstructing the early history of genome duplications in vertebrates has proven particularly difficult and remains a matter of debate.
A subsequent third round of genome duplication (3R) occurred in the lineage of teleost fishes, between approximately 320 and 350 million years ago (Meyer and Schartl 1999; Taylor et al. 2001; Dehal and Boore 2005), further expanding the repertoire of glycolytic enzymes, including some additional hexokinase paralogs. In certain actinopterygian lineages (e.g. pufferfish species from Takifugu and Tetraodon genus), a duplication of HXK1 was retained, while in others (e.g. zebrafish), the duplicated copy was lost (Postlethwait et al. 2004; Steinke et al. 2006).
The three rounds of whole-genome duplication (1R/2R/3R) that occurred early in the vertebrate ancestor profoundly shaped the evolution of the glycolytic pathway, including the diversification of the hexokinase family (Meyer and Schartl 1999; Steinke et al. 2006).
Among the 100 kDa hexokinases, the HXK3 paralog is dated to have emerged more ancestrally, during the Ordovician period. This paralog remains relatively understudied and exhibits the highest accumulation of amino acid substitutions among hexokinases, particularly within its N-terminal domain. This elevated divergence in the NTD suggests that the region may have lost its catalytic function, as observed in the human HXK3 protein (Cárdenas et al. 1998).
Interestingly, phylogenetic analysis suggests that the HXK3 gene was lost in the Cyclostomata, Chondrichthyes, and Actinopterygii lineages, being maintained only in Sarcopterygii, including Actinistia fishes and Tetrapoda. Consistent with this pattern, hexokinase family genes have already been identified and characterized in zebrafish, but no HXK3 ortholog was found (González-Alvarez et al. 2009).
This restricted phyletic distribution suggests that HXK3 may have acquired a lineage-specific function in lobe-finned vertebrates. Its absence in Cyclostomata, Chondrichthyes, and Actinopterygii groups further supports the hypothesis that HXK3 became dispensable in these lineages. Our synteny analysis of the HXK3 locus across vertebrates shows that orthologs of the genes neighboring HXK3 are conserved and remain syntenically organized even in lineages where HXK3 were lost, indicating that the genomic context was preserved despite the gene loss. However, the limited functional characterization of HXK3, even in humans, prevents a clear understanding of the physiological impact of its loss in these species.
During the evolution of the HXK gene family, another duplication event, estimated to have occurred in the Ordovician period, gave rise to the Cyclostomata-specific HXKB and an ancestral 100 kDa hexokinase that later diversified into Cyclostomata HXKA and the jawed vertebrate paralogs HXK1, HXK2, and HXK5. According to the phylogenetic analysis, orthologs of Cyclostomata HXKB appear to have been lost in jawed vertebrates. The retention of distinct HXK paralogs in jawless fishes and jawed vertebrates highlights their evolutionary relevance and suggests possible subfunctionalization or neofunctionalization events. Further exploration of the regulatory and structural characteristics of these paralogs will provide deeper insight into their roles in vertebrate metabolism and evolutionary history.
Interestingly, Nakatani et al. (2021) propose that following the 1R event shared by cyclostomes and gnathostomes, cyclostomes underwent an additional polyploidization event independent of the gnathostome-specific 2R; consequently, the possibility that this event contributed to the expansion of the HXK family in cyclostomes cannot be excluded
In jawed vertebrates, later specific duplication events led to the emergence of HXK2 and the divergence of HXK1 and HXK5, respectively. HXK1 and HXK5 are highly similar enzymes, and synteny analysis shows that they are consistently arranged in tandem across all Gnathostomata genomes. This may explain why HXK5 was the last member of the HXK family to be identified. In vertebrates, HXK isoenzymes were originally discovered and classified based on their electrophoretic mobility (González et al. 1964). Given the high similarity between HXK1 and HXK5, it is likely that HXK5 was previously misclassified as HXK1, with both enzymes being considered a single paralog.
HXK1, HXK2, and HXK5 paralogs acquired an N-terminal extension that allows them to associate with the outer mitochondrial membrane by interacting with the voltage-dependent anion channel (VDAC) (Wilson 2003). This mitochondrial localization grants them privileged access to ATP generated through oxidative phosphorylation, enhancing their catalytic efficiency (Wilson 2003). Interestingly, a similar strategy is employed by plants, which independently evolved mitochondrial-bound hexokinase paralogs through convergent evolution (Araújo et al. 2011). This suggests that the mitochondrial localization of hexokinases evolved independently during eukaryotic evolution, potentially reflecting a convergently selected mechanism for metabolic regulation.

### What is Lost is Gained Elsewhere: Divergent Evolution of Catalytic Activity of Vertebrate HXK Terminal Domains
Among the 100 kDa hexokinase paralogs in vertebrates, HXK2 is the only one in which the NTD retains catalytic activity, whereas the CTD remains catalytically active in all paralogs (Farooq et al. 2023). The loss of catalytic function in the NTD of most paralogs may have contributed to its accelerated evolutionary divergence, potentially explaining the greater sequence variability observed in the NTD compared to the CTD of 100 kDa hexokinases (Fothergill-Gilmore and Michels 1993; Cárdenas et al. 1998). This is further supported by phylogenetic analyses, which reveal a greater genetic distance in NTD relative to the CTD. Indeed, the mean genetic distances determined within the NTD and CTD groups are 0.370 and 0.280, respectively.
Our structural analysis offers a mechanistic explanation for the unique catalytic activity of the HXK2 N-terminal domain (NTD). A conserved salt bridge between Asp447 and Lys451 appears essential for stabilizing the loop that anchors Ser449, critical for substrate binding. This interaction is absent in HXK1, HXK3, and HXK5. The loss of this interaction correlates with the loss of NTD activity, a hypothesis further supported by experimental mutagenesis studies (Ferreira et al. 2021). These findings reinforce the idea that subtle structural variations, even outside the active site, can have profound functional consequences.
Comparative analysis of HXK1 and HXK5 N-terminal domain (NTD) sequences across diverse species suggests that Asp447 was likely replaced by serine early in evolution, prior to the HXK1−HXK5 divergence, and that this substitution has been conserved in extant lineages. In contrast, HXK3 NTD sequences exhibit several lineage-specific substitutions. Nevertheless, the conservation of key regulatory residues (equivalent to Asp447, Ser449, and Lys451 of HXK2) in coelacanths, amphibians, and certain reptiles indicates that functional divergence in HXK3 NTD likely occurred later and independently across distinct evolutionary branches.
The presence of substitutions at these key positions does not, by itself, provide definitive evidence for the loss of NTD catalytic activity, as other structural or regulatory factors may also contribute. However, the recurrent replacement of these residues across species supports the hypothesis that they play a central role in modulating NTD function. Taken together, these patterns highlight that changes at discrete residues, shaped by natural selection, can result in the current enzymatic properties, while also emphasizing the need for further experimental validation to fully elucidate the functional implications of these molecular changes.
Although the ionic interaction between Asp895 and Lys899 is conserved in the CTD of all vertebrate HXK isoenzymes, mutational analyses in human HXK have shown that disruption of this interaction does not affect or only partially reduces CTD activity (Ferreira et al. 2021). One of the main structural differences between the two HXK domains lies in the linker helix α13 and helix α26, located at the junctions of the NTD and CTD, respectively. While helix α13 connects the NTD to the CTD, helix α26 is not associated with any structural element and remains unbound. The preservation of the salt bridge in catalytically active CTDs across HXK paralogs suggests that this interaction contributes primarily to structural stability rather than being essential for catalytic function.
Together, these findings underscore the functional divergence between NTD and CTD. The NTD requires a tightly coordinated Asp–Lys interaction for substrate engagement and catalytic efficiency, whereas the CTD appears to be more structurally robust, tolerating perturbations to the corresponding interaction with minimal effects on activity. This divergence likely reflects evolutionary adaptations that have differentially shaped the catalytic roles of each domain while preserving overall hexokinase functionality.
As noncatalytic domains, the functional role of the NTD in HXK1, HXK3, and HXK5 paralogs remains unclear. A distinctive feature of mammalian hexokinases is their allosteric inhibition by glucose-6-phosphate, which supports the “gene duplication event theory.” This hypothesis suggests that a gene duplication event gave rise to a hemi-domain that later evolved into a regulatory binding site (Arora et al. 1993). However, the functional expression of the cDNA encoding the CTD of human HXK1 in Escherichia coli resulted in a recombinant half-hexokinase containing only one glucose-binding site and one putative ATP-binding site. Remarkably, this truncated enzyme remained catalytically active and sensitive to glucose-6-phosphate inhibition, similar to the full-length enzyme (Magnani et al. 1992). These findings challenge the gene duplication theory by demonstrating that G6P-dependent regulation can occur independently of the NTD.
Despite lacking catalytic activity, the NTD of HXK1 has been shown to bind ATP and glucose 6-phosphate, modulating the enzyme's association with mitochondria. Within this domain, a specific binding site for glucose 6-phosphate regulates the interaction of the brain paralog with mitochondria, thereby controlling the balance between its soluble and membrane-bound forms (Arora et al. 1993). However, the ability of the NTD to regulate mitochondrial binding in HXK5 remains unknown. Additionally, HXK3 does not associate with mitochondria, raising questions about the functional relevance of the NTD in this paralog.

### Adapted to the New Normal: Positive Selection in HXK Paralogs of Mammalian Lineages
Understanding the forces that shape gene evolution is essential for interpreting the origins of genetic diversity and functional innovation. In vertebrate HXK paralogs, observed gene duplications and structural differences suggest that distinct selective regimes may have shaped their evolutionary trajectory. To investigate this hypothesis, we conducted a positive selection analysis focusing on mammals.
The availability of numerous high-quality annotated genomes in mammals facilitates robust comparative analyses of hexokinase gene sequences, structures, and evolutionary patterns. Moreover, mammals exhibit a wide range of dietary strategies, metabolic rates, and glucose homeostasis mechanisms, making them an ideal group for investigating the evolutionary forces acting on hexokinase paralogs. Studying evolutionary pressures in mammals is therefore crucial for identifying signatures of selection, uncovering the molecular basis of adaptive traits, and distinguishing lineage-specific innovations from deeply conserved elements. Besides that, such data are not yet available for the different avian lineages and other vertebrate groups, making mammals a more suitable model for this type of approach.
The analysis of the HXK1 paralog, which is mainly expressed in the nervous system, indicated that positive selection appears to have occurred in the ancestral of Atlantogenata, which includes the Afrotheria and Xenarthra superorders. Atlantogenatan mammals have been shown to exhibit relatively low basal metabolic rates (BMRs), which may have co-evolved with reduced encephalization quotients (EQs) (O'Shea and Reep 1990). For instance, sirenians (Afrotheria) have remarkably low EQs: 0.27 in the Florida manatee (Trichechus manatus) and 0.38 in the dugong (Dugong dugon). These values are among the lowest recorded in extant mammals and appear to have remained relatively stable since the Eocene (O'Shea and Reep 1990). Studies on Proboscidea (elephants) reveal significant brain evolution throughout the Cenozoic era. This evolution is characterized by changes in brain size and structure, potentially linked to ecological and behavioral adaptations. Despite their large body sizes, elephants have lower EQs relative to other large-brained mammals, suggesting a different scaling of brain-to-body size relationships (Benoit et al. 2019).
Afrotherians, despite including large-bodied taxa like elephants, also tend to exhibit lower BMRs than expected for their size, suggesting lineage-specific constraints and energetic strategies (Lovegrove 2005). In the case of Xenarthra, it has been reported that this group exhibits the lowest BMRs among therian mammals (Elgar and Harvey 1987).
These systemic metabolic characteristics have implications for neural energetics. Because the brain is one of the most energetically costly organs, reductions in total energy expenditure may constrain both brain size and neural activity patterns. For instance, in sloths, extreme energy-saving strategies, such as low brain temperatures and slow nerve conduction, have been described, highlighting the link between systemic and neural metabolism (Montgomery and Sunquist 1978; Hayssen 2010). Overall, these findings suggest that the nervous systems of Atlantogenata have evolved under unique metabolic regimes and evolutionary adaptations, which may be reflected in distinct evolutionary pressures on genes related to energetic metabolism, such as HXK.
The detection of positive selection signals on the HXK2 paralog gene in the ancestral of rodents suggests an adaptive evolutionary trajectory likely associated with the specific energetic demands of these mammals. HXK2 is predominantly expressed in skeletal muscle and heart tissues (Wilson 2003). In rodents, skeletal muscle generally contains a high proportion of glycolytic fibers, specialized for rapid, high-intensity contractions fueled predominantly by anaerobic glycolysis, which is linked to high glucose consumption (Schiaffino and Reggiani 2011). Rodents also exhibit elevated basal metabolic rates relative to body size, a trait associated with their high thermogenic and locomotor activity (Careau 2013). Under such conditions, enhanced regulation or enzymatic efficiency of HXK2 might have been evolutionarily advantageous, improving glucose uptake and processing in metabolically active tissues. Interestingly, it has been shown that HXK2 expression in rodents is responsive to both exercise and fasting, suggesting a role in metabolic flexibility (O'Doherty et al. 1994; Gosmain et al. 2005). Adaptive changes resulting from positive selection could improve responsiveness to these metabolic states, contributing to greater ecological versatility.
Positive selection signals were detected in the HXK3 paralog across broad mammalian clades, such as the ancestral of Eutheria and Boreoeutheria, as well as within the ancestral of more specific lineages, including Ruminantia, Ferae (including Carnivora and Pholidota), Megachiroptera, and Eulipotyphla. The detection of positive selection in HXK3 across deep and more recent lineages of placental mammals suggests that this paralog has undergone recurrent adaptive changes during mammalian evolution. Although HXK3 is the least studied among the hexokinase paralogs, which limits definitive functional inferences, these findings point to a potentially significant and underexplored evolutionary role. It is worth mentioning that, unlike the other paralogs, HXK3 is sensitive to excess glucose and intracellular ATP levels, potentially acting as a metabolic sensor in specific cellular contexts (Cárdenas et al. 1998).
The presence of adaptive signatures in both the ancestral lineages of Eutheria and Boreoeutheria points to recurrent or sustained selective pressures acting along with the evolution of HXK3 of early placental mammals. These forces may be linked to shifts in energy metabolism, glucose sensing, regulatory mechanisms tied to placental development, thermoregulation, or increased brain energy demands, features that are particularly prominent in eutherian mammals (Thompson and Nicoll 1986). Interestingly, in the human placenta, HXK2 expression is significantly elevated during the first trimester, followed by a notable shift toward HXK3 expression in the second trimester (Prater et al 2021 ). However, the physiological implications of this paralog switch remain unclear. Such evolutionary patterns are consistent with the hypothesis that HXK3 acquired specialized roles not entirely redundant with other paralogs, possibly contributing to the evolution of more specialized metabolic networks.
Unlike monogastric animals, ruminants absorb only minimal amounts of glucose from the gastrointestinal tract into the bloodstream. This is because microbial fermentation in the rumen converts dietary carbohydrates into volatile fatty acids, primarily acetate, propionate, and butyrate, which largely supplant glucose as the principal energy substrates under fed conditions (Panserat et al. 2014). Consequently, circulating glucose levels in ruminants are markedly lower than in nonruminants, typically ranging from 2.4 to 4.5 mM in sheep (Ovis aries) and 2.3 to 4.2 mM in cattle (Bos taurus) (Arai et al 1995; Kaneko et al 2008). Ruminants also exhibit a distinct pattern of insulin secretion, with plasma propionate serving as a stronger stimulus for insulin release compared to glucose, in contrast to non-ruminant physiology (Farningham and Whyte 1993). Because little or no glucose reaches the liver, the activity of hepatic hexokinase is significantly reduced. The small amount of glucose absorbed is insufficient to induce hepatic expression of this enzyme. Moreover, the metabolic reliance of ruminants on volatile fatty acids makes glucose a relatively minor fuel source, reinforcing the necessity for continuous gluconeogenesis to meet systemic glucose demands (Panserat et al. 2014).
The detection of positive selection signals in the HXK3 paralog in Ferae mirrorder (Including Pholidota and Carnivora) suggests that this paralog may have acquired specific adaptive functions linked to the unique energy metabolism of this group. Among them, Pholidota and Carnivorous species, with diets rich in proteins and lipids but low in carbohydrates, require fine-tuned adjustments in glucose metabolic pathways. In this context, adaptive changes in HXK3 may be associated with its role in maintaining energy homeostasis under conditions of low glucose availability.
Pangolins, which belong to the order Pholidota, are natural predators of ants and termites and have a specialized insectivorous diet (Hua et al. 2015). Although data regarding the metabolism of these species remains scarce, it can be inferred that these animals have a low or even negligible carbohydrate intake.
Among the Carnivora order, cats have adapted to a diet high in protein (approximately 54% of dry matter) and low in carbohydrates (around 8% of dry matter), a direct consequence of their strict carnivorous lifestyle based on animal prey (Panserat et al. 2014). Consistent with these dietary adaptations, cats exhibit lower activities of carbohydrate-digesting enzymes in the gastrointestinal tract, reduced rates of glucose incorporation into glycogen, and prolonged glucose clearance times in glucose tolerance tests, when compared to omnivorous species such as rodents or humans (Verbrugghe et al. 2012). These characteristics suggest that cats, as obligatory carnivores, are poorly equipped to metabolize large glucose loads efficiently (Thiess et al. 2004). Moreover, felines display very low functional HXK activity in the liver, which is not inducible by dietary carbohydrates. Combined with minimal hepatic glycogen synthase activity, this makes them unable to rapidly get lower blood glucose following a high-carbohydrate meal. As a result, the feline liver appears to be functionally oriented toward glucose production and export, rather than utilization, aligning with the species' nutritional profile (Moore et al. 2012).
In contrast, canines possess an adaptive, insulin-regulated form of hepatic HXK (Hornichter et al. 1967). The presence of functional hepatic HXK activity in dogs likely reflects their dietary flexibility and the fact that canids are not strict carnivores. This enzymatic adaptability enables a more efficient metabolic response to dietary carbohydrates, supporting the broader omnivorous potential of their natural diet.
The detection of positive selection signals in HXK3 within Megachiroptera, one of the two major families of bats, suggests that this paralog may have undergone adaptive modifications in response to the distinct metabolic demands of frugivorous species. Bats from this group, such as flying foxes (Pteropus spp.), are characterized by a high-carbohydrate diet, relying primarily on fruit and nectar (Aziz et al. 2021). Notably, bats in general exhibit unusually high blood glucose levels compared to other mammals, both during fasting and postprandial states (Courts 1998; Munshi-South and Wilkinson 2010), likely due to their high-energy lifestyle and the demands of powered flight. Among them, the Megachiroptera suborder, also called megabats or “flying foxes,” predominantly consume fruits and nectar, diets that are highly rich in sugars and low in lipids and proteins (Kunz and Fenton 2003). Indeed, Frugivorous bats can exhibit blood glucose levels exceeding 750 mg/dL after sugar intake, the highest recorded in mammals (Camacho et al. 2024). Additionally, the glucose transporter 4 (GLUT4) gene (Slc2a4) has undergone adaptive changes in megabats to meet the metabolic demands of their high-sugar diet (Shen et al. 2012).
In this context, HXK3 may have been a target of positive selection to support metabolic adaptations that enable efficient glucose processing under frequent glycemic fluctuations. The adaptive changes in HXK3 could play a role in preventing glucose toxicity while maintaining energy supply, particularly in tissues being highly active during flight.
The detection of positive selection signals in HXK3 within Eulipotyphla, a diverse clade of small insectivorous mammals including moles, shrews, and hedgehogs, further underscores the potential evolutionary relevance of this paralog. However, current data on the glucose metabolism of this group remain limited, preventing robust functional inferences. While some studies suggest high metabolic rates and glucose turnover in species like shrews (Ochocińska and Taylor 2003), the physiological significance of HXK3 remains unresolved. Thus, despite the detection of positive selection, it remains unclear whether the evolutionary pressures observed are linked to dietary factors, energy demands, or other lineage-specific traits.
Altogether, the detection of adaptive signals in such phylogenetically and metabolically diverse clades, including ruminants with chronically low glycemia, felids with carbohydrate-intolerant metabolism, and fruit bats with chronically high glycemia, raises the hypothesis that HXK3 may serve as an evolutionary plastic metabolic modulator. The cumulative data suggest that positive selection on HXK3 may reflect metabolic adaptations to both hypoglycemic and hyperglycemic physiological contexts, possibly through paralog-specific regulation or tissue-specific roles in glucose sensing and utilization.
Positive selection signals in the HXK4 paralog were detected exclusively within Australidelphia, a major clade of marsupials that includes kangaroos, wallabies, and possums. HXK4 encodes the hepatic hexokinase, a low-affinity paralog predominantly expressed in the liver and essential for modulating glycolytic flux in response to systemic glucose levels (Wilson 2003). The liver's pivotal role in glucose homeostasis suggests that adaptive modifications in HXK4 may reflect unique metabolic constraints in marsupials. These animals, in contrast to eutherian mammals, exhibit distinct metabolic features such as lower basal glycemia, reduced insulin sensitivity, and alternative mechanisms for neonatal glucose regulation, owing to their short gestation periods and highly altricial young (Szdzuy and Mortola 2008; Renfree 2010). Thus, positive selection in hepatic HXK4 may represent lineage-specific optimization of hepatic and/or pancreatic glucose metabolism in response to these physiological challenges.
Despite the liver-centered role of HXK4, no evidence of altered selective regimes for this paralog was detected in ruminants or carnivores. Notably, both groups are generally characterized by very low or absent hepatic HXK activity, a trait consistent with their dietary strategies: ruminants rely on volatile fatty acids rather than glucose, while obligatory carnivores, like felines, consume diets with minimal carbohydrate content (Kaneko et al. 2008; Verbrugghe et al. 2012). However, HXK4 remains functionally expressed in the pancreas in these taxa, contributing to glucose-stimulated insulin secretion. The absence of positive selection in HXK4 in these groups may reflect the reduced hepatic reliance on this paralog, rather than a lack of functional importance in other glucose-sensing tissues.

## Conclusion Remarks
Our comprehensive evolutionary analysis of the HXK gene family across Metazoa provides novel insights into the origin, diversification, and adaptive significance of vertebrate hexokinases. The 100 kDa hexokinases, exclusive to vertebrates, appear to have originated from domain duplication and fusion events in early vertebrate ancestors. Subsequent duplications led to the emergence of paralogs with distinct catalytic and regulatory properties.
Molecular dating and synteny analyses support a stepwise expansion of the gene family, with duplication events coinciding with key transitions in vertebrate evolution, including the emergence of jawed vertebrates and the diversification of tetrapods. Structural comparisons revealed that the functional divergence between NTD and CTD domains is a defining feature of vertebrate HXKs. Among these, only HXK2 retains catalytic activity in both domains, while HXK1, HXK3, and HXK5 have accumulated lineage-specific substitutions that suggest regulatory or neofunctionalized roles rather than catalysis.
Selection analyses focusing on mammals uncovered paralog- and lineage-specific signals of positive selection. These results highlight how metabolic strategies, such as dietary specialization, thermoregulation, or tissue-specific glucose demands, have exerted selective pressure on different HXK paralogs throughout vertebrate evolution.
In summary, our findings demonstrate that vertebrate hexokinases have been shaped not only by ancient gene duplications but also by ongoing evolutionary forces acting on structural domains, expression patterns, and catalytic functions. The interplay between gene architecture, regulatory adaptation, and selective pressure reflects the central role of HXKs in metabolic innovation.
Given their pivotal role in energy metabolism, future studies should explore how the evolutionary diversification of HXKs contributes to functional specialization in tissues and organisms, as well as their implications in biomedical contexts such as cancer metabolism, insulin resistance, and metabolic disorders like diabetes.

## Materials and Methods

### Phylogenetic and Genetic Distance Analysis
For the phylogenetic analysis, protein sequences encoding HXK from species representative of different taxonomic groups, including Amoebozoa, Fungi, and Metazoa, were retrieved through BLAST searches in the NCBI database using sequences from human, zebrafish, and yeast as bait, using default parameters. The last search was performed in the second half of 2025 and the sequenced retrieved are indicated in Table S1.
The sequences were aligned using the MUSCLE tool (Edgar 2004 ) implemented in MEGA11: Molecular Evolutionary Genetics Analysis version 11 (Tamura et al. 2021) with default parameters. All alignments used in this work are available in Supplementary File S1. The final dataset was used for phylogenetic tree reconstruction by the maximum likelihood method under the best substitution model selection in IQ-TREE software (Nguyen et al. 2015), with 1,000 replicates of ultrafast bootstrap and the approximate likelihood-ratio test (aLRT) statistics. The genetic distance of phylogenetic groups was also computed in MEGA11 using the p-distance method. All phylogenetic archives are available in Supplementary File S2.

### Gene Duplication Identification and Time-Calibrated Estimation
Gene duplication events were identified using the “Find Gene Duplications” tool in MEGA11: Molecular Evolutionary Genetics Analysis version 11 (Tamura et al. 2021). A DL (duplication and loss) model was also used to compute the reconciliation likelihood using the EVAL strategy in GeneRax (Morel et al. 2020). The species tree used for reconciliation was retrieved from TimeTree (Kumar et al. 2022). The reconciled gene tree was used to estimate duplication times using the RelTime-ML algorithm implemented in MEGA11, using the substitution model selected by IQ-TREE2. Calibration of divergence times was performed by applying fossil-based age constraints at multiple phylogenetic nodes, following Benton et al. (2015). All calibrated nodes were inferred as speciation events by both MEGA11 and GeneRax. The calibration points were provided as minimum and maximum constraints in million years ago: Metazoa (550.25–833), Bilateria (550.25–636.1), Protostomia (550.25–636.1), Deuterostomia (515.5–636.1), Ambulacraria (515.5–636.1), Chordata (514.0–636.1), Olfactores (514.0–636.1), Sarcopterygii (408.0–427.9), Craniata (457.5–636.1), Vertebrata (457.5–636.1), Osteicthyes (420.7–444.9), Actinopterygii (378.19–422.4), and Tetrapoda (337.0–351).

### Structural Analysis
Comparative structural analyses of the N-terminal (NTD) and C-terminal (CTD) domains of hexokinase 2 (HXK2) and hexokinase 1 (HXK1) were conducted using their respective crystal structures (PDB IDs: 2NZT and 2F9O), along with AlphaFold-predicted structures of hexokinase 5 (HKDC) and hexokinase 3 (HXK3). All structural comparisons were carried out using UCSF Chimera v1.14 (Pettersen et al. 2004). For primary sequence comparisons, the translated protein sequences were aligned using the MUSCLE algorithm (Edgar 2004) implemented in MEGA11 (Tamura et al. 2021) with default parameters, and the resulting alignments were visualized using the BoxShade interface.

### Detection of Episodic Diversifying Selection in Mammalian Sequences
To investigate signatures of episodic diversifying selection in mammalian hexokinase sequences, a dataset comprising orthologous protein-coding sequences of human Hexokinase from at least 470 representative mammalian species was obtained from the TOGA database (https://genome.senckenberg.de/download/TOGA; Kirilenko et al. 2023).
The sequences were aligned using the MUSCLE algorithm implemented in MEGA11: Molecular Evolutionary Genetics Analysis version 11 (Tamura et al. 2021) with default parameters. A phylogenetic tree was reconstructed for each HXK alignment, using the maximum likelihood method in IQ-TREE, applying the best-fit substitution model with 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates and the aLRT statistics.
Detection of episodic diversifying selection was performed for each HXK alignment using the Adaptive Branch-Site Random Effects Likelihood (aBSREL) method (Smith et al. 2015) available on the Datamonkey webserver (http://www.datamonkey.org/absrel; Pond and Frost 2005 ). All branches were selected as test branches.
