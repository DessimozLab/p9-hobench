Project description: Your goal is to build a comprehensive and informative literature-based benchmark for assessing the quality of hierarchical orthology inference. The benchmark should include selected species’ annotated genomes to represent a set of test gene families retrieved from literature, as well as metrics of quality for the result of orthology inference. Benchmarking the quality of an orthology inference method is complicated by many biological phenomena. Some gene families went through a complex history of duplication and loss events. Incomplete lineage sorting and horizontal gene transfer further complicate evolutionary history in some cases. Different evolution rates and promiscuous domains can confuse inference methods into splitting the same gene family into multiple ones, or merging unrelated families. Ancient whole genome duplications complicate near-root resolution of duplication/speciation events. Annotation errors, errors in species tree inference, data contamination, alignment errors – all this further creates sources of error that contribute to the process of orthology inference. An informative benchmark of the quality of orthology inference should therefore be aware of these and other sources of error, contain data examples with these phenomena, and implement metrics that represent the method’s ability to be robust to those. Besides that, it should cover different taxonomic ranges over different parts of the tree of life, taxonomic levels, speed of evolution, and complexity of the underlying evolutionary history.

Existing benchmarks in the Quest for orthologs benchmark and OrthoBench families do not cover enough the diversity of gene families and taxonomic diversity. Moreover, assessing the accuracy of hierarchical orthology (or, equivalently, the accuracy of reconciled gene trees) is more nuanced than assessing the accuracy of pairwise one-to-one orthology or strict orthologous groups. The benchmark therefore should not only provide the data, but also the metrics that are relevant and informative for hierarchical orthology specifically.

Data collection: create a curated compendium of reference orthologous groups derived from publications which describe the phylogeny of “well-known” families. Suggest and implement a plan of how to find relevant publications, retrieve the necessary information from them, and assemble data in a unified format. You should identify relevant papers, search them and retrieve their text and relevant associated data, and extract the relevant evolutionary information from text, figures, and supplements. Examples of extracted information might be sequences, trees, protein structures, cross-references and accession numbers, metadata about where particular gene families are believed to have undergone duplications, etc.

Continue working on the project even if the requested human input is not provided. 

Metrics of inference quality: Suggest metrics of orthology inference accuracy that are based on the information extracted from the literature – for example, average number of correctly assigned sequences per species, or how often a method’s prediction agrees with the literature, or whether or not it predicted correctly an evolutionary event discussed in the literature. A metric should provide a measure that, if maximized by a certain method, allegedly would indicate an improvement of the method’s ability to predict hierarchical orthology.
Make a simple python wrapper for running orthology inference tools (e.g. fastoma, orthofinder) that produce hierarchical orthology on the prepared data and computing the metrics. You can judge the difficulty of the selected data by how well the methods perform on these data – and how much they agree – in addition to theoretical considerations based on the literature.
